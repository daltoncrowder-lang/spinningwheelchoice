## How the result is chosen

An online wheel doesn't spin like a physical one. When you press spin, the page asks the browser for a random number. That number is converted into an angle, and the animation is calculated to slow down and stop at that angle. The spinning motion is for show. The outcome is decided by the random number.

Browsers provide random numbers through built-in functions. Many wheels use the standard random function, and some use the browser's cryptographic random source, which is designed to be unpredictable even for security purposes. For choosing dinner or a classroom volunteer, either is far more random than any human could be.

## Equal slices mean equal odds

If a wheel has eight equal slices, each one covers one eighth of the circle, so each has a 12.5% chance of being picked. If one option appears twice, it covers two slices and has twice the chance. That is the only way a fair wheel changes the odds: by changing how much of the circle an option covers.

## Why fair wheels feel rigged

People expect randomness to look evenly spread out. Real randomness doesn't. With four options, the same result coming up three times in a row happens about once every 16 spins. Spin often enough and you'll see it, and it will feel suspicious even though it's exactly what probability predicts.

This is the same effect that makes people believe a coin is "due" for heads after several tails. The coin has no memory, and neither does the wheel. Each spin starts fresh.

## How to check a wheel

- Make sure every option has a slice of the same size.
- Look for duplicate entries you didn't mean to add.
- If you need everyone to get exactly one turn, remove each result after it is picked instead of relying on randomness to spread picks out.
